The origin of cosmic rays of the highest energies
S S Al-Dargazelli, A W Wolfendale, A Smialkowski and J Wdowczyk
 Physics Department, University of Durham, Durham, UK  Physics Department, University of Lodz, Lodz, Poland
Received 11 June 1996, in final form 19 August 1996
Abstract. Recent work has focused on the presence of anisotropies in cosmic ray arrival directions for energies above 2 × 1019 eV. Here, we examine the results in some detail from the standpoint of identifying likely extra-galactic sources. Of the types of galaxy studied, those in collision, and which are within 10 Mpc of the Milky Way, are the strongest contenders.

1. Introduction

In a series of papers (Szabelski et al 1986, Chi et al 1992a, b, c, d and later papers) we examined the world's data on cosmic rays above 1018 eV from the standpoint of searching for their origin. We concluded that there was evidence for preferred directions in space (cosmic ray clusters) for their arrival directions--presumably the directions to specific sources. There were more clusters near the Galactic Plane than expected by chance and this fact, coupled with an apparent general excess towards the Galactic Plane (below 2×1019 eV), the Galactic Plane enhancement of Wdowczyk and Wolfendale (1984), led us to claim that there were still some cosmic rays (CRs) of Galactic origin of energy as high as 2 × 1019 eV and perhaps a little beyond. An increasing fraction of extragalactic (EG) particles (presumed to be protons) above a few 1018 eV was presumed to be responsible for the rest of the particles. Only the VIRGO cluster seemed to be favoured as a source of EG particles (Szabelski et al 1986).
Very recently analyses have been made which have updated the earlier work by including more recent observations and by analysing the arrival directions in a different way. Stanev et al (1995) have examined the arrival directions from Northern Hemisphere arrays for particles above 2 × 1019 eV and claimed a non-uniform arrival direction distribution. Above 4 × 1019 eV it is claimed that there is a correlation with the `supergalactic plane', a region where there are many potential EG sources.
Hayashida et al (1996) have studied the data from their own extensive air shower array (AGASA). They, too, found concentration on the supergalactic plane, but only for a subset; they also detect some interesting groupings.
In what follows we endeavour to determine the nature of the sources of at least some of the EG particles. A necessary, and new, analysis is made of the topography of the magnetic field in the Galactic Halo and beyond; only if this is known will it be possible to interpret the anisotropies.

2. Evidence for cosmic ray anisotropies
2.1. Large-scale anisotropies
Our earlier claims for a Galactic Plane enhancement and thus a Galactic origin of some of the particles (see section 1) seem, to us, to be still valid although its amplitude is somewhat smaller than first thought. The amplitude does in fact still grow with increasing energy, just as expected (see Chikawa et al (1995) for a very recent summary). The fact that the enhancement is never really strong is put down to two factors: a dominant flux of Galactic

2.2. Clusters
Positive proof of the existence of specific astronomical objects will only come when `hot spots' or `clusters' (the terminology here) appear in the CR arrival direction at the highest energies and these coincide with a priori likely sources. In earlier work, we (Chi et al 1992a) made a statistical analysis of arrival directions and put forward tentative claims for clustering (i.e. hot spots) in various directions. Figure 1(a) shows the map. A cluster was such that four particles had to have energy above 1 × 1019 eV and be within 6 of a particle of energy above 3 × 1019 eV. Of greatest interest to us was the apparent excess (over isotropy) of clusters near the Galactic Plane; in the present work, however, our interest is in the region away from the Plane.
As mentioned in the introduction, later workers have carried out somewhat similar analyses but with more data. In this context, a particularly useful analysis has been made by Stanev et al (1995); these authors have confined attention to the Northern Hemisphere arrays and their results are presented in figure 1(b). The most significant hot spots are shown as the darkest areas on the map and the least significant are dashed. The energy threshold is 2 × 1019 eV.
There have been new data presented from the Akeno array (E > 4 × 1019 eV) by Hayashida et al (1996) and although these data have been analysed from the standpoint of correlations with the Galactic and Supergalactic Planes they have not been examined with respect to clusters in general. However, the authors drew attention to three unusually close pairs and these are indicated in figure 1(a). These observations are important because the (earlier) Akeno data were not included in our own earlier analyses.

3. Discussion
3.1. Clusters within 20 of the Galactic Plane
Of the seven clusters (or, more properly, `regions of excess', since two are of considerable angular extent) four are within 20 of the Plane (regions 4-7) and it is reasonable, but by no means certain, to associate them with Galactic objects. The lack of certainty is associated with the lack of obvious astronomical counterparts. Pulsars, unidentified, as yet, at other wavelengths are a possibility but the mechanism by which such objects accelerate particles to 1020 eV is quite unclear.
Until the Galactic sources are identified (if, indeed, they are Galactic) then there must be doubt as to whether or not the other sources (responsible for the `regions of excess') are Galactic or not. However, the large angular extent of some of the sources militates against a Galactic explanation, at least if the particles are protons. For iron nuclei even the high-latitude sources would need to be more than about 1 kpc away in order to reproduce the large extents of figure 1(b). Furthermore, we would expect the highest particles of all to centre on the regions of excess. Although this may be happening in regions 2, 3 and 6 it is not happening for the extended regions 1 and 4.

3.2. Clusters/regions beyond |b| = 20

The most reasonable explanation for these clusters and indeed for most `high'-latitude, high-energy particles is that they come from extragalactic (EG) sources (hardly an original suggestion!). In fact, those with |b| < 20 could also be EG but unidentified because of the near impossibility of detecting EG objects of significance in this region--a fact stemming from Galactic obscuration. We present the clusters and regions of excess (all to be referred to, still, as `clusters') in figure 1(c) denoted A, B and C.
In what follows we examine the various contenders for EG sources, but before doing this it is necessary to examine the likely magnetic field topology in both Galactic and EG space. The question of loss of particles by interactions with the 2.7 K cosmic microwave background (CMB) will also be discussed.

4. Factors affecting propagation
4.1. The magnetic field topography
Of crucial importance to the propagation of cosmic rays is the topography of the magnetic field, characterized by its mean value and its reversal length, the latter being a characteristic dimension which encapsulates what is no doubt a complex spectrum of irregularities. In fact the encapsulation is energy dependent in the sense that particles of a particular energy, E, are affected by irregularities on the scale of the Larmor radius L appropriate to that energy. Thus, the problem hinges on the power contained in the irregularity spectrum, on a scale of order L. At low energies where most of the total energy is carried, there is often a close linkage between the irregularities (turbulence) and the cosmic ray spectrum and there are, for example, plasma equations which relate L to the conditions of the medium but these are not appropriate for use at very high energies. Instead, rather empirical methods must be adopted.
Locally, within the Galactic Disk, the mean smooth field is commonly regarded to have a strength of  2 µG (e.g. Phillipps et al (1981), Chi and Wolfendale (1990)) and a variety of observations (Faraday rotation, stellar polarization, etc) have led to rather direct studies of the field irregularities. These yield a reversal length of  200 pc, (Osborne et al 1973).
Starting with the average strength of the magnetic field an estimate can be made using the well known empirical fact that there is (often) a near equality of the overall magnetic field energy density and that in cosmic rays (e.g., B  3 µG corresponds to the CR energy density, locally, of  0.5 eV cm-3). In what follows we assume that this equality is valid everywhere.
Proceeding outward we are guided by synchrotron radiation analyses (e.g. Phillipps et al 1981) to postulate that the field falls to, typically, 1 µG at z = 1 kpc and then more rapidly. Although not of great importance here, we adopt the Giant Halo model of Moore and Davies (1994) in which the Halo extends to at least 100 kpc. The (ionized) gas temperature is estimated to be  2 × 106 K and the density, at  65 kpc,  5 × 10-5 cm-3. The corresponding energy density is  10-2 eV cm-3 yielding a field of  0.15 µG when equipartition arguments are adopted. Figure 2 shows the situation.
Derivation of the reversal length  is much more difficult but an attempt has been made using the value ( 0.2 kpc) appropriate to the Galactic Plane. The fact that this value comes from rather direct observations means that there is considerable power in the irregularity spectrum at this  and we presume that there will be, similarly, power in the scaled value--which is of relevance to high-energy cosmic ray propagation.

4.3. Interactions with the CMB
The details of the interaction of protons with the CMB have been worked out by many authors. The mean free path is equal to the (adopted) distance to VIRGO of 15 Mpc at 3 × 1020 eV. Allowance for the extra path due to diffusion and the enhanced radiation field due to emission from the galaxies in the VIRGO cluster will reduce the energy to somewhat below 2 × 1020 eV.

5. Potential sources of EG particles within 10 Mpc
5.1. General remarks
There are many possibilities. These include the following. (i) Galaxies which are 'normal' in other respects; insofar as we do not know the nature
of the sources of very energetic particles in our own Galaxy we cannot define `cosmic ray normality'.
(ii) Galaxies with active galactic nuclei (Seyferts, and Active Galactic Nuclei (`AGN') in general).
(iii) Galaxies which are colliding. The lack of appreciation of what factor indicates which galaxy should emit very energetic particles means that cuts on optical brightness, strength of radio emission, etc may not be appropriate although a priori it would be expected that `brighter' galaxies would be stronger contenders. In what follows we examine the maps of galaxies of various types and compare them with the CR excess map of figure 1(c). As to a distance scale, we adopt H0 = 75 km s-1Mpc-1.

6.1. General remarks
Inspection of table 1 shows that protons from sources beyond 10 Mpc--and perhaps as far as 20 Mpc--might just give detectable large area clusters for mean energies above 4×1019 eV. Thus, we now search these regions.
6.2. All galaxies in the range 10-15 Mpc
Figure 6 shows maps for two distance ranges. We note that all the clusters have virtually no galaxies in their areas. An interesting consequence of this fact is that the magnetic field in line of sight to A, B and C may be somewhat lower than in our estimate of figure 2 (and  correspondingly longer).
6.3. All galaxies in the range 15-20 Mpc
The situation here is very different for clusters A and B. Here, there are very many galaxies, potential sources, in the areas. 7. Discussion
7.1. Colliding galaxies Inspection of the maps of the potential EG sources considered shows that there is a distinct possibility that colliding galaxies are responsible (figure 5), the case for cosmic ray cluster `C' being particularly strong. The colliding galaxy pair VV 338 (N672 and U1249) is therefore most favoured. The `local' colliding galaxies towards clusters A and B are not

quite as strong and it is just possible that these are not (mainly) responsible, as will become clear shortly. It is premature to work out a detailed model to account for the particles, suffice it to say that a spectrum of the form E-2 and total rate of energy production of approximately 1040 ergs-1 would be sufficient. Such an energy could come from magnetic connection of the field lines of the galaxies; it should be possible to accelerate protons within 108 y (the lifetime for 2 × 1020 eV protons on the CMB) and allow them to escape the accelerating region. Those of much lower energy may well be trapped and never escape; this fact is not important, however, because in this region Galactic particles predominate.
It can also be remarked that there is direct evidence for enhanced magnetic fields in interacting galaxies (e.g. Hummel and Beck 1996) and also a possibility related phenomenon--the presence of intense bursts of massive star formation (Jog and Solomon 1992).

7.2. Galaxies in the VIRGO and URSA MAJOR clusters
In terms of sheer galaxy numbers these two regions are clear favourites for clusters A and B. Thus, if otherwise normal galaxies emit very energetic protons from time to time then these two galaxy clusters are favourites. Alternative, and perhaps more likely, explanations are the following.

7.2.1. Colliding galaxies. Insofar as we have made a strong claim for cluster C being due to nearby colliding galaxies and figure 5 shows other nearby colliding galaxies to be close to or within the clusters, colliding galaxies must be taken very seriously.
Figure 7(b) shows that there are also colliding galaxies in the regions of clusters A and B. Indeed, because of the high density of galaxies in the VIRGO and URSA MAJOR galaxy clusters there are probably more present--and capable of accelerating ultrahighenergy cosmic rays--than so far catalogued. In terms of statistics the greater number of potential colliding galaxies in VIRGO and URSA MAJOR compared with the local ones probably outweighs the loss by the inverse square law and extra diffusion en route to the earth.

7.2.2. Cluster shocks. Another mechanism that must be taken seriously is the acceleration
of particles in `cluster shocks'. Kang et al (1995) have examined this process in some detail
and proposed that cosmic rays of ultrahigh-energy can be produced in VIRGO from which the very highest energy ones (E 1019 eV) could reach the earth. A problem is that the
acceleration mechanism is slow so that losses on the CMB limit the maximum to the CMB cut-off at  6 × 1019 eV. Kang et al need an efficiency for conversion of inflowing energy to cosmic ray energy of  10%; not an unreasonable fraction. An attractive feature of the mechanism is that the shell from which the particles escape has an angular radius of about 35, which is not far from the semi-longitude extent for cluster A (but bigger than that in
latitude).
In order for this mechanism to have relevance here, the acceleration time (T ) must be
reduced. It is set by the strength of the magnetic field in the shock region, B, and velocity of shock, µs: T  B-1 µ-2p, where p is the particle momentum. Kang et al adopt B = 1 µG and u = 3000 km s-1. It is just possible that higher values of B and u can be taken which would allow particles of energy up to 1020 eV to be generated but anything
higher would require unreasonable values. It is interesting to note, in this context, that the very highest energy particles (E > 2 × 1020 eV) avoid VIRGO although one is close to the CANIS MAJOR region.

8. Conclusions
Quite a strong case can be made for a significant flux of extragalactic particles from nearby (R < 10 Mpc) colliding galaxies with perhaps some energetic particles from the VIRGO and CANIS MAJOR galaxy clusters; colliding galaxies are again a possibility in these galaxy clusters.
In such a model most of the CRs above 1019 eV would, in fact, come from Galactic sources. The idea of a giant Halo, adopted here, would allow sufficient trapping of these particles were they heavy nuclei (iron?). It is true that the Fly's Eye group (Bird et al 1993a, b) claim that most particles above 1019 eV are protons, and thus EG, but we would draw attention to work with the Akeno array (Yoshida et al 1994) which does not support the idea of any change in composition from the mixed composition determined by them
