Abstract. There are a number of radioactive `clocks' in the cosmic radiation that can be used
to measure the time scales for cosmic ray processes in the Galaxy. With high-resolution isotope
measurements available from ACE it is now possible to read these clocks with greatly improved
accuracy and address key questions about the origin and lifetime of cosmic rays. This paper discusses
the status of three such investigations.

                                       1. Introduction

It is now generally agreed that the only sources capable of providing the 3 ×
1040 ergs per second necessary to sustain cosmic rays in the Galaxy are supernova
(SN) explosions. It is also generally accepted that the mechanism by which the bulk
of cosmic rays are energized is shock acceleration - the same mechanism that ap-
parently accelerates most of the energetic particles that originate in the heliosphere.
Beyond this, there remain several outstanding questions as to the origin of cosmic
rays. They include: What is the origin of the material that gets accelerated? Do
cosmic rays originate from fresh supernova ejecta, `old' interstellar material, or
a combination of these? Are cosmic rays accelerated by a `one-shot' mechanism
(i.e., by a single SN shock), or by distributed acceleration continuing over their
lifetime (i.e., a series of SN shocks)? What volume of the Galaxy do cosmic rays
fill? Does it include a galactic halo as well as the disk?

   These questions are all related to the time scales of cosmic-ray processes in the
Galaxy, including the elapsed time between the nucleosynthesis of cosmic-ray ma-
terial and cosmic-ray acceleration, the time scale for the acceleration process(es),
and the time scale over which cosmic rays are stored in the Galaxy. Radioactive
isotopes in cosmic rays provide a means of measuring these time scales.

   Cosmic rays can be divided into two general classes. Primary cosmic rays are
those that survive after being accelerated from the original pool of source material.
Secondary cosmic rays are produced by nuclear interactions of (mostly) primary

   In 1997 the Advanced Composition Explorer (ACE) was launched carrying a
suite of instruments designed to measure the composition of energetic particles
with much greater precision than was possible previously (Stone et al., 1998a).
Included was a Cosmic Ray Isotope Spectrometer (CRIS) that has achieved excel-
lent mass resolution (m = 0.1 to 0.25 amu for 2  Z  30) with the collecting
power ( 250 cm2sr) needed to resolve even rare species (Stone et al., 1998b). In
this paper we discuss three examples of cosmic rays clocks for which considerable
progress has been achieved with ACE data, and consider the implications of these
new measurements for our understanding of cosmic ray acceleration and transport
in the Galaxy.

                          2. Acceleration-Time Delay Clocks

Some time ago, Soutoul al. (1978) identified three electron-capture nuclei in the
Fe-group (59Ni, 57Co, and 56Ni), all products of explosive nucleosynthesis, that
essentially decay only by electron-capture (EC). Since cosmic rays with >500
MeV/nucleon spend most of their time as fully-stripped nuclei, a significant frac-
tion of these nuclei would be expected to survive if cosmic rays are accelerated
to high energies soon after nucleosynthesis. Soutoul et al. (1978) suggested that
the abundances of 56Ni, 57Co, and 59Ni, with electron capture half-lives ranging
from  6 days to  105 yr., could provide a measure of the time delay between
nucleosynthesis and cosmic-ray acceleration. It is evident that 56Ni has decayed in
cosmic rays because the dominant Fe-peak isotope is 56Fe, as in the solar system.
The more interesting cases are 57Co (T1/2 = 0.74 y) and 59Ni (T1/2 = 7.6 × 104 y).
The Ni and Co isotope measurements in Figure 1 (Wiedenbeck et al., 1999) show
that there is very little 59Co in cosmic rays, suggesting that a reasonably long time
delay has occurred.

   However, we must also consider the possibility that a significant fraction of the
mass 59 nuclei were synthesized as 59Co instead of 59Ni. Figure 2, from Wieden-
beck et al. (2000) indicates that the time delay must have been  3 × 104 years as
long as at least 20% of the mass 59 material originated as 59Ni, and 105 years
if at least 40% of the mass-59 nuclei originated as 59Ni. Woosley and Weaver
(1995) have calculated the yields of Fe-group isotopes from Type II supernova. For
30  R. A. MEWALDT ET AL.

stellar masses ranging from 11 to 25 solar masses, the fraction of mass 59 nuclei
originating as 59Ni ranges from 27% to 87%. For a Salpeter initial mass function
it is found that  68% of the mass 59 material originates as 59Ni (Wiedenbeck
et al., 1999). It therefore appears that only time delays >105 years are allowed

unless cosmic rays come from a very narrow range of stellar masses (in which case

isotope anomalies would be expected in other Fe-group elements).
   A time delay of 105 years between nucleosynthesis and acceleration is incon-

sistent with models in which supernovae accelerate their own ejecta. It is con-

sistent with models in which cosmic rays are accelerated from the interstellar

medium (e.g., Olive and Schramm, 1982), or from a low-energy seed population

pre-accelerated by stellar flares (Meyer, 1985). One would also expect a long time

delay in models in which the refractory elements in cosmic rays originate from

interstellar dust grains (Meyer et al., 1997). Higdon et al. (1998) have suggested

that most cosmic rays originate in super-bubbles in which a series of SN explosions
extend over periods of  105 years or more. In this case, for 59Ni to decay before
acceleration, it is necessary that the ejecta from SN early in the life of the super-
bubble remains relatively un-mixed with the surrounding ISM for at least  105
years before being accelerated to high energies by shock waves from subsequent

SN.
   It is also possible that cosmic ray 59Ni nuclei could pick up electrons and decay

in-flight if they manage to retain these electrons for a time comparable to the
EC-decay lifetime of 105 years. The average energy of the CRIS measurements
is  300 MeV nucl-1, corresponding to an average of  400 to 500 MeV nucl-1
outside the heliosphere. Wiedenbeck et al. (2000) show that at energies of
450 MeV nucl-1 or more the electron attachment mean free path (mfp) is several
g/cm2, while the electron stripping mfp is < 10-2 g cm-2. At these energies one
does not expect appreciable 59Ni decay for the mean density  0.3 to 0.4 cm-3 indi-
cated by the 10Be and other beta-decay clock measurements (see below). However,

if the observed cosmic rays have spent an appreciable time at lower energies (i.e.,
<200 MeV nucl-1, where the electron-capture mfp is <1 g cm-2) before getting
accelerated to higher energies, it is possible that a significant fraction of the 59Ni

could have decayed in-flight. The question of whether cosmic rays are accelerated

in a step-wise fashion (also called re-acceleration) is discussed below.

 3. Electron-Capture Nuclei as Probes of Cosmic Ray Transport Processes

Over the  104 year lifetime of a single SN blast wave it is theoretically possible
to accelerate cosmic ray nuclei to  1014Z MeV, where Z is the atomic number
(see, e.g., Gaisser, 1990). However, it is also possible that most cosmic rays gain
their energy from a series of SN shock waves. One possible way to address this
question is with measurements of electron-capture nuclei that would be expected
to decay if the observed cosmic rays have spent an appreciable fraction of their

lifetime at lower energies where electron attachment is much more likely. Nuclides
that can probe these processes include 7Be, 37Ar, 44Ti, 49V, 51Cr, 55Fe, and 57Co,

   Connell and Simpson (1999), Connell (2001) and Soutoul et al. (1998) have
concluded that a significant fraction of 49V and 51Cr have decayed, based on com-
parisons of Ulysses and Voyager measurements of the 49V/51V ratio with a leaky-
box model (LBM) of cosmic-ray propagation in the Galaxy. They conclude that
some re-acceleration has occurred.

   With the large collecting power of CRIS it is possible to examine the abun-
dances of EC-nuclei in several energy intervals. In the results of Niebur et al.
(2000), shown in Figure 3, there appears to be a clear deficit of the EC-nuclide
51Cr at lower energies, and a corresponding overabundance of the daughter 51V.
When the sum of 51Cr and 51V are compared with stable secondary species there is
no observed energy dependence. Similar results are found for the parent-daughter
combination 49V and 49Ti (Niebur et al., 2000). These results support the conclu-
sion that the observed variation of these ratios with energy is due to the energy-
dependence of electron-capture and loss processes rather than some unexpected
energy dependence of Fe-fragmentation cross sections.

   In contrast to the findings of Connell and Simpson (1999) and Soutoul et al.
(1998), the energy dependence of the 51Cr and 51V abundances in Figure 3 (and
also those of 49V and 49Ti; see Niebur et al. (2000), appear to be reasonably well
accounted for without invoking reacceleration. In addition, Webber (2000a) con-
cludes that the 51V/49V ratio measured by ACE is consistent with a LBM without
reacceleration. Webber (2000b) has also compared ACE and Ulysses data with a
Monte Carlo diffusion model and finds that the 51V/51Cr data favor reacceleration,
while the 49Ti/49V data do not.

   Observations of EC isotopes in cosmic rays are still in their infancy, as are
models that interpret them. Their abundances depend on the energy dependence of
the electron-capture and loss cross sections and the fragmentation cross sections,
on the history of cosmic ray acceleration and possible re-acceleration, on inho-
mogeneities in the interstellar matter traversed by cosmic rays (Raisbeck et al.,
1975; Letaw et al., 1984), and on energy changes that occur as cosmic rays enter
the heliosphere. It is therefore premature to draw firm conclusions from these new
observations without examining thoroughly the effects of uncertainties in modeling
these processes. However, it is already clear that these data will be the subject of
lively debate over the next few years.

        4. Beta-Decay Clocks and Cosmic-Ray Transport in the Galaxy

The lifetime of cosmic rays in the Galaxy is important because it determines the
power required to sustain the energy density of cosmic rays in the Galaxy (e.g.,
Gaisser, 1990). This lifetime can be derived from the measurements of radioactive
isotopes such as 10Be (half-life of 1.5 My). The first reliable observations of 10Be
in cosmic rays occurred during the 1970s, when it was discovered that most 10Be
produced in cosmic-ray interactions has decayed, leading to an estimated confine-
ment time of esc  10 to 30 My for cosmic-ray storage in the Galaxy (see review
in Simpson and Garcia-Munoz, 1988). Taking into account that cosmic rays with
several hundred MeV amu1 have traversed  5 g cm-2 of interstellar material, this
lifetime suggests that the mean density of the medium that cosmic rays inhabit is
 0.2 to  0.4 H atoms cm-3, substantially less than the mean density commonly
ascribed to the galactic disk ( 1 H atom cm-3). This has led to suggestions that
cosmic rays must spend appreciable time in low-density regions such as the galactic
halo.

accurately measure and compare abundances of four separate beta-decay clocks

   One goal of this study is to determine the characteristics of regions where cos-
mic rays reside. In the LBM he cosmic-ray intensity and ISM density are uniform
in space and time, and the surviving fractions of various species are sensitive only
to the mean density of the storage region. There are now more realistic models,
including diffusion models in which cosmic rays also have access to a galactic halo
or other low-density regions (e.g., Webber et al., 1992; Strong and Moskalenko,
1998; Ptuskin and Soutoul, 1998; Streitmatter and Stephens, 2000). In such models
the distance (R) from which radioactive species can arrive is limited by its half-life,
such that R  ( DTd)1/2, where D is the diffusion coefficient, Td is the mean life
for radioactive decay at rest, and  = 1/(1 - 2)1/2. In this picture the abundance
of a given radioactive clock is sensitive mainly to conditions within a sphere around
the Sun whose radius scales as R.

   Figure 7 illustrates schematically the size of the sphere sampled by five radioac-
tive superimposed on a representation of the density profile of the galactic disk.
Note that the 10Be measurements shown here reflect conditions out to  400 pc,
well beyond the disk, while the shorter-lived species probe shorter distances. Al-
though there are as yet no reliable measurements of 14C in cosmic rays, any ob-
served 14C can originate only in very nearby regions. The only approach currently
available to extend this study to greater distances is to extend 10Be measurements
to energies above  1 GeV amu-1 where relativistic time-dilation effects become
important.

several radioactive clocks in the 1 to 20 GeV amu-1 region using abundance ratios

Taken at face value, their results suggest that the surviving fraction of 10Be and
other clock nuclei beyond 2 GeV amu-1 is less than expected in the LBM. However

in an energy range where there are few measurements, and cross section differ-
ences of  20% (as seen, e.g., in Yanasak et al., 2001) would lead to significantly
different conclusions. With direct measurements of relevant cross sections at 1 to
10 GeV amu-1, this approach might prove fruitful.

                                         5. Summary

This paper has summarized the first measurements of cosmic ray clocks from the
CRIS instrument on ACE and discussed the implications for some key questions
38  R. A. MEWALDT ET AL.

in cosmic ray astrophysics. The decay of 59Ni in cosmic rays implies that super-
novae do not accelerate their own ejecta; rather, there must be a time delay 105
years between the time of nucleosynthesis and acceleration. New measurements of
49V and 51Cr provide evidence for energy-dependent electron-capture decay, which

may provide a new tool for studying energy-changing processes in the Galaxy and

the heliosphere. Finally, ACE has now provided well-resolved measurements of
four radioactive clocks (10Be, 26Al, 36Cl, and 54Mn) with greatly improved statis-

tical accuracy. The observed abundances can be fit with a steady state leaky-box
model characterized by an escape lifetime of 14.5 ± 1.5 My and a mean density of
0.36 H atoms cm-3. However, these results are also consistent with a diffusive halo
model characterized by a diffusion coefficient of  2 × 1028 cm2 s-1. Although
it is perhaps still too early to conclude that the clock has run out on the leaky box

model, the focus of most theoretical efforts seeking to interpret the new, high pre-

cision observations is now directed towards more sophisticated models of cosmic

ray transport.
